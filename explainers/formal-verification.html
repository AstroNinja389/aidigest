What does it mean to prove a program correct?

The short version: usually programmers check a program by trying it out on many examples. “Formal verification” is a stricter method: you prove, the way a mathematician proves a theorem, that the program can never break a certain rule, and a computer checks every step of the proof.

Testing versus proving

Imagine checking a lock. Testing is trying a thousand wrong keys and seeing that none of them opens it. That is reassuring, but the next key might still work. Proving is studying the mechanism and showing that no key except the right one can possibly turn it. A proof covers every case at once, including the ones nobody thought to try.

Who checks the proof?

Proofs about real programs run to millions of steps, far too many for a person to read. So they are written in a special, very strict language (one of the best known is called Lean), and a small, simple program called a proof checker goes through them line by line. If a single step does not follow from the ones before, the checker rejects the whole thing. This is why it matters less who wrote the proof, a person or an AI: an AI can make things up, but a made-up proof does not get past the checker.

Why it is rarely done

Writing such proofs is slow, expensive work for rare specialists, often many times more work than writing the program itself. Until now it has been done mostly for small, critical pieces of software. The hope is that AI can take over the laborious part.

What a proof cannot promise

A proof only shows that the program obeys the rules that people wrote down. If the rules are incomplete, or describe the wrong thing, the proof will pass and the danger will remain. Deciding what to prove is still a human job, and the place where mistakes can hide.
