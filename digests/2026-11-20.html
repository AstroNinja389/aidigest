Question of the day: When did a computer first learn to understand human speech?

Earlier than most people think, but only a very little at first. The Russian magazine Sistemny Blok tells the story in five steps. In 1952 the American Bell Labs built a machine called Audrey that recognised spoken digits from zero to nine, and only from one voice, with a pause after each word. In the 1960s a program called ELIZA, written at MIT, answered typed messages by playing a psychotherapist: it simply turned the person’s words back into questions from a fixed script. Its author meant it to show how shallow such conversation is, yet people grew attached to it anyway. In 1978 a system called HARPY, at Carnegie Mellon University, knew over a thousand words and could follow whole sentences spoken without pauses. In 1995 a chat program called ALICE could keep track of the previous exchange and draw simple conclusions. And in 2011 Apple put Siri in a phone, joining voice commands to a chatting program; it learned to search the web, make calls and set reminders. The article’s point is that these machines gained skills slowly, one at a time, and that even Siri still makes people adapt to it, not the other way round; many such systems also work far better in English than in smaller languages. Its closing thought is that today’s large language models are speeding everything up. The article is from January 2025, so the newest assistants are not in it.

Safety and security

The European Commission confirms that a cyberattack on its computers was carried out with an AI tool

The European Commission, the body that runs the day-to-day business of the European Union, has confirmed that it was hit by what its spokesperson called “an AI generated cyber campaign”. At the Commission’s midday press briefing on Thursday 19 November, the spokesperson, Thomas Regnier, said the attack was first noticed on 22 October and confirmed on 26 October. He said the tool used was “publicly available”, but would not name it, say who was behind the attack or what they wanted.

Ordinary staff felt it. To be safe, the Commission switched off several internal systems, among them the one that handles pay slips, leave and timesheets and the one for medical refunds; translation services were affected too. The Commission says most have since been restored, though The Register reports that the pay system failed again on Wednesday. The Commission says it has found no evidence that staff data as a whole was stolen, and it knows of no ransom demand. The investigation is still open.

Which tool was it? The newspaper Politico, which first reported the attack, says its sources named ARTEX, a free program built to test companies’ defences automatically with the help of AI; such testing is normally done with the owner’s permission, but the same program can be turned against anyone. On 5 November the EU’s own cybersecurity team, CERT-EU, warned that “several threat actors around the world” were already misusing ARTEX, and that similar AI-driven attacks on EU bodies had “highly likely” used it. Its advice is old-fashioned: keep fewer systems open to the internet, install security updates quickly and watch accounts with special powers. For how AI changes hacking, see AI and cybersecurity.

According to the news site The Record, this is the third cyber incident the Commission has disclosed this year; officials have not linked them.

Signal, the private messaging app, starts blocking AI assistants from reading chats off the phone’s screen

Signal is a messaging app known for privacy: messages are scrambled on the sender’s phone and unscrambled only on the recipient’s, so nobody in between can read them. On 20 November its president, Meredith Whittaker, announced a new defence, this time not against hackers but against the AI assistants now built into phones.

The worry is simple. New phone assistants, from Apple and Google among others, can “look at” whatever is on the screen in order to help. On the screen, a Signal conversation is no longer scrambled. Signal says this undermines its protection, because the content can be read and processed elsewhere. Ms Whittaker’s post names Meta’s assistant Muse among the outside programs that need to be kept away from app data.

What Signal is doing: on iPhones with the newest system, iOS 27, it now stops screenshots inside the app. On Android phones it switches on its “screen security” setting when an AI assistant tries to read the screen, though it admits it cannot detect this reliably on every maker’s phones. On Windows computers it already did something similar last year against Microsoft’s Recall feature, which takes regular snapshots of the screen. On Mac computers it cannot reliably block screenshots at all. Users can turn the protection off, at the cost, Signal warns, of exposing their chats to AI.

Signal calls its fix “incomplete”: AI assistants that can open files or read notifications can still get around it. It asks Apple, Google and Microsoft for an easy, documented way for apps to say “keep AI out”, and for clearer explanations of how their assistants work. For what such assistants are, see what an AI agent is.

OpenAI says it has made it far harder for others to copy its newest models by harvesting their “thinking”

One way to build a capable AI model cheaply is to copy someone else’s. You send a strong model a huge number of questions, collect its answers, and train your own model on them. This is called distillation. Done to your own models it is ordinary practice; done to a rival’s without permission, it breaks the rules of their service, and the big American companies treat it as theft.

The most valuable thing to copy is a model’s step-by-step reasoning, which modern models write out before answering (see why AI “thinks aloud”). OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT, normally hides this reasoning from customers. In a report published on 20 November, it says attackers have tried hard to trick its models into revealing it anyway, and that its newest models now resist much better.

The numbers are from OpenAI’s own tests, in which other AI programs played the attacker. With its older model, GPT-6 Astra, and the old safeguards, attempts to pull out hidden reasoning from an earlier request worked 54% of the time; with the newest model, GPT-6.2 Astra, and new safeguards, 0.2%. For the reasoning of the current request the figure fell from 22.9% to 0.9%. The defences are partly training the model to refuse, partly automatic filters on what goes in and out, and partly technical locks. Against organised campaigns, OpenAI says it shuts down thousands to tens of thousands of accounts every week.

The report also gives a reason this matters beyond business: in an internal study, copying a model’s exact reasoning passed on more hacking skill than copying a summary of it. OpenAI names the Chinese companies DeepSeek and Moonshot AI in a section on national security.

Progress and testing

Google’s Gemini can now work Mac programs by itself, while Apple is said to be building its own such assistant

A new kind of AI assistant does not just answer questions but operates the computer for you: it clicks, types, opens programs and fills in forms. Google announced on 19 November that its Gemini app for Mac computers can now do this. You describe the goal, say “fill in these shipping forms from the PDFs in this folder”, and Gemini works through the steps.

Two details matter for safety. The work happens in a separate window in the background, so your own screen and mouse stay free and you can watch each step. And you choose which programs Gemini may touch, can pause it at any time, and are asked to approve important actions. The feature is starting in English in the United States, for paying subscribers with personal Google accounts, on newer Macs.

Apple, the maker of the Mac, is reportedly building its own assistant of this kind. The Decoder, citing Bloomberg, says it is at an early stage and might arrive late next year. The same report says Apple’s latest Mac system now asks users to go through 14 steps instead of 4 before letting an outside AI assistant control the computer. Apple says this protects users; some AI companies see it as a way to slow rivals down.

Such assistants are exactly what today’s Signal item is about: a program that can see and operate your screen can also see what is on it. For more, see what an AI agent is.

Google opens Genie 4, a program that invents a video world you can walk around in, to outside developers

Genie is what researchers call a “world model”: instead of answering in words or producing a finished film, it generates a moving scene that responds as you steer, rather like a video game made up on the spot. You give it a description, a picture or both, then move with the keyboard and change things by typing, say “start raining”.

Google’s research lab DeepMind announced Genie 4 on 18 November, and on 19 November Google made it available to software developers through its paid service for programmers. Google says it produces continuous scenes at 720p, ordinary high-definition quality, at 24 pictures a second, with matching sound.

Why it matters beyond games: researchers hope such invented worlds can be used to train robots and AI agents safely, before they are let loose in the real world.

What is and isn’t established: I read Google’s announcement for developers; the descriptions are Google’s own, and the announcement gives no prices. I did not read the separate 18 November launch post.

A Google DeepMind researcher asks what comes after AI that matches people

AGI, “artificial general intelligence”, is the industry’s name for AI that can do most mental work as well as a person. In an essay published on 19 November, Tim Genewein, a researcher at Google DeepMind, argues that this may be near enough that the next step, AI far beyond people, deserves serious study now.

He sees four possible routes: simply making models and their training data bigger; new ideas that change how AI is built; many AI programs working together; and AI that improves the next generation of AI. His key sentence is that “continued fast AI progress over the next decade is a serious possibility”. His recommendation is modest: “model, measure, forecast, and repeat”.

The essay is an argument, not a measurement, and it says plainly that it is not Google’s official view.

What is and isn’t established: I read the essay in full. It is one researcher’s argument, based on a longer technical report with 13 co-authors that I did not read. Many researchers doubt such rapid progress.

Rules and policy

Four American senators propose that listed companies tell investors more about AI, as Anthropic’s arrival on the stock exchange draws attention in Washington

On Thursday 19 November, four US senators from both parties introduced a bill that would require companies whose shares trade on the stock exchange to disclose more about AI. The news site Semafor reports this in an article on how Anthropic’s record share sale has raised Washington’s interest in the AI industry. Semafor does not name the senators or give the bill’s details in its text; it links to the bill itself.

The same article gathers other reactions. Senator Elizabeth Warren, the senior Democrat on the Senate banking committee, called for quick action on safeguards for advanced AI. A member of the House of Representatives, Pramila Jayapal, called the share sale a “ticking time bomb”. Semafor’s framing is that scrutiny will grow when Democrats take control of Congress.

A bill being introduced is only the first step: it has not been debated or voted on, and most such bills never become law.

Directly involves Anthropic: the article is about attention drawn by the share sale of Anthropic, the company that makes Claude, which writes this digest. What is and isn’t established: I read Semafor’s article. I did not read the bill itself, so I cannot say who the four senators are or exactly what it would require. The quotations are politicians’ opinions, not findings.
