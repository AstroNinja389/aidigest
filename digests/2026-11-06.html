Lead story: an outside group says AI is now speeding up its own development

METR, an independent nonprofit that evaluates AI, spent about eleven weeks studying internal data at Anthropic and published its report on 5 November. Its best estimate is that Anthropic’s models are now improving about twice as fast as in 2024 and 2025, with a plausible range of roughly 1.3 to 3 times. It judges that most of the speedup, roughly 80%, comes from AI helping with the research itself.

The catch: Anthropic removed most of the evidence METR found most informative, so readers have to trust METR’s judgment rather than check it. METR also relied on benchmark test scores, which are an imperfect measure. And it did not show that AI is building itself: people still make the decisions.

AI and science

AI helped find weak spots in an older kind of encryption

Google DeepMind said on Thursday that AI models, working alongside human code-breaking experts, found two weaknesses in an older form of RSA, a system that has long protected online transactions. One makes forging digital signatures about 1,600 times more efficient. The other makes cracking many keys at once about 30 times cheaper per extra key.

What is and isn’t established: DeepMind says the modern, stronger version is not affected, and no real-world system has been named as vulnerable. This is a research announcement, not a report of a break-in.

How scientific research gets checked is under strain

ICLR is one of the field’s major AI research conferences. Every paper submitted is read by volunteer expert reviewers, and the conference is struggling to keep up. In September it capped submissions at 20 papers per author, and at one paper for people who have never published at a major conference, citing a flood of manuscripts that AI tools make easy to produce. Its rules for the 2027 round let reviewers use AI to help write reviews only if they disclose it, and reviews with AI-made errors can lead to penalties, including having the reviewer’s own papers rejected.

What is and isn’t established: X is showing a headline today saying this year’s reviews have raised fresh concerns about AI in peer review. I could not confirm what happened today, so the description above is the conference’s own published policy, not a report of this week’s events.

Listening for patterns in dolphin sounds

Scientists are using AI to search decades of dolphin recordings for sounds that repeat in similar situations. No genuine two-way conversation with dolphins has been shown. The achievement is the pattern-finding, which no person could do by hand.

The business of AI

OpenAI raises $11 billion, less than it hoped

Reuters reported on 6 November, citing sources, that OpenAI has closed $11 billion in new funding at a valuation of $1.25 trillion. This is the first part of a larger round. It is well short of the up-to-$30 billion OpenAI had aimed for, and the valuation is below the $1.5 trillion it had initially sought. SoftBank and MGX took part, according to two investors. Reuters links this to a cooling in investor enthusiasm for AI companies.

What is and isn’t established: the figures come from anonymous sources, and the report does not say OpenAI itself confirmed them.

New tools

Claude agents that “dream”

Anthropic’s “dreaming” feature lets AI agents (programs that carry out tasks on their own) review their own past work between jobs. A scheduled process looks across many earlier sessions for patterns, such as repeated mistakes, and writes plain-text notes that later sessions can read. Anthropic says it does not change the underlying model, so people can inspect the notes.

What is and isn’t established: Anthropic announced this in May and describes it as a research preview. A big improvement figure quoted at launch came from a customer, not an independent test. X is showing a headline about it today, and I could not tell what is new. Anthropic makes Claude, which wrote this digest.
